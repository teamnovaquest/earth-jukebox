Sound details

One signal (0 to 100) drives every sound: higher is brighter, faster and higher in pitch; lower is darker, slower and lower, with more echo and crackle. The mapping is a design choice, not a physical law.

Every frame in this mode comes from NASA's Earth Information Center (EIC) gallery, section "Daily Visualizations", made by NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio for the EIC hyperwall. The page wraps each EIC frame on the globe, reads the colour at your pin against NASA's own colour bar for that visualization, and turns the reading into sound.

About this data (read before citing)
  • Pictures: NASA GIBS (EOSDIS) tiles. Visible Earth combines up to three satellites (Suomi NPP, NOAA-20, NOAA-21) on the same day to fill gaps; any area still empty shows NASA Blue Marble and is reported as partial coverage.
  • Music numbers: NASA POWER monthly data, which come from the MERRA-2 reanalysis (a model that assimilates satellite and ground observations), not direct measurements at your pin. Whole Earth uses NASA GISS GISTEMP.
  • Climate signal: a prototype sonification score invented for this project, not an official NASA product and not a measure of ecological health. 50 = this place's 1981-2010 normal (Whole Earth: 1951-1980).
  • Colours versus warming: picture colours show actual temperatures, which swing by tens of degrees between seasons and places. A degree or two of warming over decades is nearly invisible in them. The graph and the music carry the long-term trend.
  • EIC mode data: every frame, colour bar and reading in EIC mode comes from NASA's Earth Information Center gallery (svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/gallery/earth-information-center), section Daily Visualizations. Copies sit in the eic folder because NASA's server does not let other websites use its images on a 3D globe; get_eic_frames.py records each frame's original NASA address in eic/manifest.json. Readings are taken from the frame's colours using NASA's colour bar, so they are approximate.
  • EIC gallery datasets and how each is read: vegetation (SVS 5544), air temperature (5147), PM2.5 (5151), CO (5153), NOx (5154), ozone (5152), wind (5148), humidity (5150), sea surface temperature (5101), precipitation now (4285), precipitation compared with the season (4897) and active fires (5113). Humidity, sea surface temperature and fires are taken from NASA's videos, so their colours are slightly less exact. Ozone is matched on how purple the colour is; calm wind and light ozone are uncoloured on NASA's maps and are reported as below the scale; rain readings are NASA's colour classes; the fire reading is relative brightness, because NASA's fire scale has no numbers. Daily sea ice (5046, 5064) is not included: NASA shows it only as a tilted globe view that cannot be placed accurately on this globe.
  • Confirmed EIC dataset: collection ct-ch4-monthgrid-v2025 appears in NASA's Earth Information Center data repository (NASA-IMPACT/eic-data, pull request 21: "Successfully published"). It is listed first in EIC mode and tagged "EIC confirmed".
  • EIC mode: frames and values come live from NASA's VEDA catalogue (openveda.cloud), the data services behind the EIC dashboard. Values are read at your pin from the same data as the picture. Raw units are shown as NASA publishes them; see each dataset's description. NASA's VEDA documentation (docs.openveda.cloud, VEDA Dashboard page) says Earth.gov uses these same services, but the catalogue also serves other dashboards, so not every listed dataset is necessarily shown on earth.gov.
  • Analytics: every number in an analysis is calculated by Earth Jukebox from NASA data under your pin (NASA POWER since 1981, NASA EIC gallery maps, earth.gov) with Sen's slope and the Mann-Kendall test. The SI (Google Gemini) checks it against the independent ERA5 record, reads Wikipedia for regional context, and writes the explanation and suggested actions; it is not allowed to change or add numbers. Suggestions are general guidance, not professional advice. Analyses are saved only on your device.
  • Map view: the coloured data layer on the map is the same NASA frame as on the globe, re-projected exactly from NASA's latitude-longitude grid to the web map grid. When you zoom in closer, NASA GIBS and VEDA send sharper tiles of the same date. The background map (NASA Blue Marble, then Esri satellite imagery when zoomed in close), roads and place names come from NASA, Esri and OpenStreetMap and carry no climate data. The "NASA data layer" slider sets how strongly the NASA data covers them.
  • Updates: the EIC gallery frames are re-checked against NASA by get_eic_frames.py (every 3 hours on GitHub, or every hour with --watch on your computer). An open page looks for newer frames every 10 minutes, and for newer earth.gov data every 30 minutes, and carries on from the same date when they arrive.
  • Finding places: when you ask the SI Assistant to put your pin somewhere, the place is looked up in OpenStreetMap (data © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL) with the Nominatim search service, and Photon by komoot when Nominatim has no match. The SI never makes up coordinates. If an exact street or building is not on OpenStreetMap, the assistant says so and pins the surrounding area instead.
  • Moon facts: the "People and the Moon" list follows University of Kansas News (8 December 2023).

The Moon

About 384,400 km from Earth. Drawn closer and larger than true scale.

The same face of the Moon always points at Earth. With almost no air, weather or plate tectonics, marks left on its surface last a very long time. Drag to look around, scroll to zoom, click Earth in the sky (or press Esc) to go back.

People and the Moon

Scholars at the University of Kansas have proposed a "Lunar Anthropocene": a new epoch in the Moon's history marked by human activity (KU News, Dec 2023).

  • First impact: people first disturbed Moon dust on 13 September 1959, when the Soviet Luna 2 probe hit the surface.
  • Debris: more than a hundred spacecraft have touched the Moon, leaving spacecraft parts, scientific equipment and bags of astronaut waste.
  • Footprints: Apollo footprints and rover tracks are treated as an archaeological record worth preserving.
  • Thin exosphere: the Moon has no real atmosphere, only a delicate layer of dust and gas, and rocket exhaust from landings can spread through it.
  • Polar ice: exhaust gas can also reach the ice inside permanently shadowed craters.

What NASA has seen change

NASA does not publish a day-by-day Moon time-lapse like it does for Earth. These are individual before and after finds by the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO).

  • LRO's camera team reports at least about 1,000 new impact craters and around 100,000 other surface changes since 2009 (reported figure). LROC
  • The largest new crater found so far is about 222 m wide and formed in 2024. LROC
  • Russia's Luna 25 crashed on 24 Aug 2023 and LRO imaged the new crater. NASA
  • A spent SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage hit the Moon in August 2026, and LRO imaged the new crater. NASA Science
  • Spent Apollo rocket stages left craters about 35 to 40 m wide. NASA
